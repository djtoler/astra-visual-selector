# YEAR SEVENTEEN — narration script, revision 4

Same structure and same words as revision 3. What is added is an intent record under each
beat, written by the person writing the script, at the time they write it.

**The discipline.** Each record says only what a later reader could not recover from the
narration itself. If the sentence already says it, it does not go in the record. Fields are
omitted when there is nothing non-obvious to put in them. Nothing here is drawn from a
controlled vocabulary, and nothing here names a template, a family or a tag. The moment
these become dropdowns they are a tag bank, and the writing starts bending toward the
library.

**The three fields.**
*Takeaway* is what the viewer should come away with, as distinct from what the sentence
says. *Must be true* is what has to survive into the visual or the beat fails. *Would be a
lie* is what a visual must not imply, including by accident.

---

## CYCLE 1 — THE TAP

### PROVOKE

Seven hundred times a second. That's how often one rapper's catalog gets played on Spotify,
and by the time the day closes out it comes to fifty-nine million.

Curren$y was on the 2009 XXL Freshman cover, and every song he has ever put on the platform —
the mixtapes, the Jet Life run, the features, all of it stacked end to end — adds up to what
this one catalog moves in twenty-four days.

Ninety-three rappers, one dot each. Higher dot, more plays per day. Start at the bottom.

Down here, catalogs that barely register at this scale. Keep climbing and you hit names you
know, working rappers with real followings, and you climb straight past them. Near the top,
Future. Travis Scott.

Then one dot with open air under it. More than both of them added together.

> **Takeaway** — The climb should feel long before it ends, and the gap at the top should
> read as a break in the pattern rather than the last step of the ramp.
> **Must be true** — One dot per rapper, height is plays per day. The Curren$y figure is a
> duration equivalence, his entire catalog against twenty-four days of the other, not a
> ratio between two catalogs.
> **Would be a lie** — Naming the top dot. The whole cycle depends on withholding it, and a
> label here kills the next two beats.

### PREDICT

Name goes on in a second. Before it does, guess something else. A catalog pulling that much
every day, in 2026, sitting on top of everybody. What year of his career is this?

Pick a number.

> **Takeaway** — The viewer should commit to a number out loud or in their head. This is the
> only beat that asks for an answer before it has given any reason to expect one.
> **Must be true** — The question is which year of a career, not which calendar year.
> **Would be a lie** — Any career timeline on screen. A visible span lets the viewer count to
> the answer instead of guessing.

### REVEAL

Seventeen.

> **Takeaway** — The number should feel wrong against what was just shown.
> **Would be a lie** — Decoration. One number, held long enough to land.

### RECONCILE

So Far Gone came out in 2009. This chart covers the year that ends on its seventeenth
anniversary.

Year seventeen of a rap career is anniversary-tour territory. Deluxe reissues with three
unreleased demos on the back. A festival slot at seven-forty, before the headliner. Nobody's
seventeenth year is supposed to look like the top of the daily chart with two of the biggest
names in the business stacked underneath it and still coming up short.

There are three ways out of this, and anyone who has spent five minutes arguing about rap
already has all three loaded. Big back catalog. Half of it is other people's songs. Seventeen
years to pile it up.

Fair. Each one gets run on this same chart, one at a time, and I'm stamping the card whether
it helps him or not.

The daily number is the tamest thing here.

> **Takeaway** — The three objections are reasonable and the piece is going to take them
> seriously. The viewer should feel this is a test being run, not a defense being mounted.
> **Must be true** — Three distinct objections, each tested separately later. The 2009 date
> is what anchors the seventeen.
> **Would be a lie** — Playing the anniversary-tour images as mockery. They describe the
> normal case, and several artists in this piece are living it.

## CYCLE 2 — ONE SONG?

### PROVOKE

Nearly every rapper on that chart has one song carrying the building. That's the normal shape
of a career. Latto's biggest is half of everything she's ever streamed. Jay Rock's is
fifty-two percent. Ab-Soul's, seventy-one. Biz Markie, seventy-nine. One record lands and it
feeds the catalog for a decade.

So the easy read on the top dot is a couple of monsters doing the work.

> **Takeaway** — Concentration is the normal, healthy shape. These four are instances of a
> rule, not a leaderboard.
> **Must be true** — Each percentage is that artist's own biggest song against that artist's
> own total. Four different denominators.
> **Would be a lie** — Ranking the four against each other, or putting them on a shared axis.
> Fifty-two and seventy-nine are not competing numbers.

### PREDICT

You've got the shape now. Call it. What share of Drake's streams is his single biggest song?

> **Would be a lie** — Showing the shape of his catalog before the answer.

### REVEAL

Three percent.

His ten biggest songs together come to fifteen percent. The next-flattest catalog at anything
close to this size is Future, at twenty-four. His top five are under ten percent, the lowest
concentration in the set.

Run the actual test, and run it on everybody. Take each artist's single biggest song off the
platform. Re-rank.

Ab-Soul falls twenty-eight places. Latto falls thirteen. Jay Rock, twelve. Macklemore keeps
seventy-three percent of his streams and drops three spots.

Drake loses four point four billion streams, keeps ninety-seven percent of his total, and
finishes exactly where he started.

> **Takeaway** — Two separate movements. First that the catalog is flat. Then a test being
> actively run on everyone, with a before and an after.
> **Must be true** — The test removes each artist's own biggest song, a different song per
> artist. The result is places moved, not streams lost. For Drake both readings have to
> survive at once: a very large absolute loss and almost no share loss.
> **Would be a lie** — Implying he lost nothing. He lost four point four billion. The point
> is that the ranking did not notice.

### RECONCILE

He has thirty songs past a billion streams. Thirty-five of the ninety-three rappers on the
opening chart have zero. And eighty-one of the ninety-three don't have thirty billion streams
across everything they've ever recorded, which means the billion-stream songs on their own
outweigh eighty-one entire careers.

Take his ten biggest. Then take the single biggest song from each of the next five artists and
add those five together. His ten win, twenty-one billion to fifteen.

First card stamped. There's no one song you can delete to make this go away.

> **Takeaway** — Abundance. Thirty has to read as *many*, which it only does against the
> thirty-five who have none.
> **Must be true** — Thirty is a count of songs clearing a per-song bar. The eighty-one
> clears a different bar entirely, lifetime catalog rather than per song. Two measures, two
> units. The ten-against-five comparison is his ten biggest songs against five other artists'
> single biggest each, not against their catalogs.
> **Would be a lie** — Any shared axis between the thirty-five and the eighty-one, or any
> overlap drawn between those two groups. Nothing in the source says how they intersect.

## CYCLE 3 — WHOSE RECORDS?

### PROVOKE

In 2010, XXL offered him a spot on the Freshman cover. He turned it down.

The rappers on that cover, and on the one the year before, came up on the same clock he did.
2009: Kid Cudi, B.o.B, Wale, Curren$y. 2010: J. Cole, Nicki Minaj, Wiz Khalifa, Big Sean,
Nipsey Hussle, Jay Rock, Freddie Gibbs. Same era, same starting line.

One rule, applied to everybody. A record you lead, or share the lead on equally, counts in
full. A feature or a remix verse counts at half.

That's the 2010 class under the rule. Nicki at thirty billion. Cole at twenty-nine. Stack all
seven end to end.

Ninety-seven billion. Seven careers.

> **Takeaway** — A peer group starting together, not a ranking. The rule should feel neutral
> because it is about to be used in both directions.
> **Must be true** — The rule is stated before any number appears under it. The halving is
> proportional and should be visible as such, since "counts at half" is itself a proportional
> claim. Ninety-seven billion is a sum of seven careers.
> **Would be a lie** — Any suggestion he appeared on either cover. He turned it down, and
> that is the reason he is not in the group.

### PREDICT

One more bar. Read the label. Drake, lead and equal co-lead records only — nothing he guests
on, nothing off anybody else's album, just the records with his name first.

Where does it stop? Past Nicki, or past Nicki and Cole together?

And hold onto that rule. It's the same rule for everybody, which means it can be pointed the
other way, and later on I'm going to point it.

> **Takeaway** — Two jobs. Commit to where the bar stops, and register that a promise has
> been made and left open.
> **Would be a lie** — Paying the promise off here, or hinting at which direction it goes.
> The debt is collected in cycle 5 and has to stay open until then.

### REVEAL

Ninety-six and a half billion. His own records, nothing else, are the whole class. Seven
rappers with everything they have ever made against one man with only the songs that carry his
name first, and it comes down to a coin flip.

That's the class. Now put the same slice against the names near the top of the opening chart,
full catalogs, everything at full credit. Travis Scott, sixty-seven billion. Kendrick,
fifty-seven. Post Malone, fifty-six. Future, fifty-four.

The slice clears every one of them.

Extend the bar. Features, at half.

A hundred and eighteen billion. Same rule on the others puts Post Malone at fifty-two, Travis
at fifty-one, Kendrick at forty-nine.

> **Takeaway** — Near-parity should read as uncomfortable rather than triumphant. It is a
> coin flip, and the piece says so.
> **Must be true** — The two sides are deliberately unequal: his is a restricted slice, theirs
> is everything. That handicap has to be visible or the comparison looks like cheating.
> **Would be a lie** — Giving the two sides equal visual weight without showing the
> restriction. Equally, presenting the second comparison as the same test; it changes what is
> being measured.

### RECONCILE

Seven of every ten streams in the catalog sit on his own records.

> **Takeaway** — A rest. One figure, no elaboration, held briefly and let go.
> **Would be a lie** — Building this into a climax. Cycle 4 needs the room.

## CYCLE 4 — THE ACCUSATION

### PROVOKE

He lives on features. That's the charge, and it has been the charge since about 2013 — that
the catalog is a tour of everybody else's albums, that he shows up for sixteen bars on a song
somebody else built and walks off with the streams.

> **Takeaway** — The accusation should sound correct while it is being made. A viewer who
> believes it should feel represented, not set up.
> **Would be a lie** — Visual sarcasm. No scare quotes, no undercutting while the charge is
> being stated.

### PREDICT

Nobody making that case needs me to prompt them. They already have the number, and it's a real
one: a hundred and eighty-one guest verses. That's a lot of other people's songs.

So don't argue with it. Take the accusation at full strength and count the verses.

> **Takeaway** — The number belongs to the critic here, not to the narrator. This is the one
> prediction in the piece the viewer is not asked to make.
> **Would be a lie** — Presenting a hundred and eighty-one as the narrator's discovery or as
> a rebuttal. At this moment it is the prosecution's evidence.

### REVEAL

A hundred and eighty-one. The charge is true.

Future has done two hundred thirty-two. Young Thug, three hundred and two.

Fewer verses than either of them, and more streams than anyone: forty-three billion off
features, roughly two hundred forty million a verse. Half credit hits the top and the bottom
of that rate, so it cancels out. The number holds either way.

> **Takeaway** — The ground shifts rather than the claim collapsing. He does do a lot of
> features. That was never the interesting part.
> **Must be true** — Verse counts and stream rates are different quantities and must not share
> a scale. The per-verse rate is the load-bearing figure, not the verse count.
> **Would be a lie** — Presenting "fewer verses than Future and Thug" as the refutation. It
> is context. The rate is the answer.

### RECONCILE

So he does live on features, in the sense that he has done more of them than almost anyone
worth comparing him to, and they have earned more than anyone else's. Both things are true at
once, and only one of them was ever the accusation.

Throw out every guest verse he ever recorded and he's still bigger than anyone in rap with
everything they've got. Second card.

> **Takeaway** — Two true things held together without one cancelling the other.
> **Would be a lie** — Resolving this into a clean win. The charge stands; it just does not
> mean what it was meant to mean.

## CYCLE 5 — WHERE IT TURNS

### PROVOKE

Now I point the rule the other way, the way I said I would.

The 2010 class was seven rappers. Add the 2009 cover to it — Kid Cudi, B.o.B, Wale, Curren$y —
and you get eleven, all of them under the same full-credit-and-half-credit rule he just walked
through.

> **Takeaway** — The viewer should recognise the callback and feel the debt being collected.
> **Must be true** — Eleven is seven plus four, and it is the same rule as cycle 3, unchanged.
> If the rule looks adjusted, the result is worthless.

### PREDICT

If you've been waiting through all of this for the number that doesn't go his way, here it is.
Eleven careers, one rule, no thumb on the scale.

Say whether he clears them.

> **Takeaway** — The skeptical viewer is about to be right, and should be invited to say so
> before they find out.
> **Would be a lie** — Telegraphing the outcome. This is the one prediction the piece intends
> the doubter to win.

### REVEAL

He doesn't.

Together they edge him by about three percent.

> **Takeaway** — It goes against him, plainly and without cushioning.
> **Must be true** — Three percent is a real margin and has to read as a real margin.
> **Would be a lie** — Any framing that shrinks it, or that reaches for a consolation figure
> in the same breath.

### RECONCILE

Eleven careers to one, and it's close. Three percent is close enough that a different cutoff
date or a different call on two or three co-lead credits moves it, and I'm not going to
pretend the margin is doing more work than it is. But it went the other way, and that's the
only place in this whole run where it does.

No card for this one. It stays exactly as it landed.

> **Takeaway** — Unresolved, and visibly so. This is the only beat in the piece that files no
> verdict, and the absence should be noticeable.
> **Must be true** — The fragility of the margin is acknowledged without being used to throw
> the result out.
> **Would be a lie** — A stamp, a check, a tally mark, any closure graphic at all. The
> running card count established since cycle 1 has to visibly skip here.

## CYCLE 6 — ONLY THE OLD STUFF?

### PROVOKE

A song from 2015 streams in 2026 whether the artist gets out of bed or not. Nothing decays.
Nothing has to be maintained. So the top of the daily chart in year seventeen could be a very
large archive on autoplay while the man himself stopped mattering somewhere around Views, and
none of the tests so far would have caught it. The concentration test wouldn't. The class
comparison wouldn't. The features count wouldn't.

There's one place it shows up. New records either chart or they don't.

> **Takeaway** — This objection is genuinely unrefuted at this point, and the viewer should
> feel the previous three tests were not enough.
> **Must be true** — The three named prior tests are the ones actually run in cycles 2, 3 and
> 4, and none of them addresses currency.
> **Would be a lie** — Playing it as a strawman. It is the strongest remaining objection.

### REVEAL

Eighteen straight years with something on a US chart. One other rapper in the set has that
streak — J. Cole, off the cover he turned down.

Then the number ones.

Number one as a lead single. Eleven times.
Number one as an album. Nine.
Number one as somebody else's guest. Four.
Number one as a mixtape. Three.
Number one as a joint album. Two.
Number one as a compilation. One.

Thirty releases at number one on a US chart. Future and Kendrick, the next two in the set, have
twenty-six between them.

> **Takeaway** — Unbroken presence first, then breadth of format. The list should accumulate
> rather than arrive whole. This beat carries no predict, so the reveal has to do the work of
> making the viewer notice they had assumed something.
> **Must be true** — Thirty is the sum of six release types. The six are categories of
> release, not chart positions and not a sequence in time. Twenty-six is two artists combined.
> **Would be a lie** — Ordering the six as a ranking or a timeline. They are independent
> counts that happen to descend.

### RECONCILE

That kills the version where he went quiet and the archive kept running without him. It does
not tell you how much of today's fifty-nine million is new songs and how much is old ones, and
nothing in this chart can, so the third card goes down half-stamped and stays that way.

> **Takeaway** — Partial, and the hole stays visible for the rest of the piece.
> **Would be a lie** — Full closure. The half-stamp is literal and the coda returns to it.

## CYCLE 7 — THE TWO KINDS

### PROVOKE

Rappers on this platform tend to come in one of two kinds. The ones who carry their own records
— Post Malone, forty-nine billion on his own songs against six as a guest. XXXTentacion,
thirty-five and six. Kendrick, forty-one and sixteen. And the ones who live on other people's
hooks. Ty Dolla $ign, five billion on his own records and twenty-two as a guest. Young Thug,
eleven and twenty-one. 21 Savage, sixteen and twenty-three.

Album artists and feature artists. You're one end of that spectrum or you're the other, and the
ends don't mix.

> **Takeaway** — The spectrum should feel solid and obvious, because the next beat breaks it.
> **Must be true** — Every artist carries two values in the same unit, own and guest. The two
> groups are separated by which of a member's two values is larger. There is no benchmark in
> this beat and no threshold.
> **Would be a lie** — Treating these as two threshold groups. The shape resembles it and it
> is not it; nothing here is being compared against a bar. Also, the six are examples of two
> patterns, not a ranking of six.

### PREDICT

Two floors, fixed before the names go in. Thirty billion on your own records. Twenty billion on
everyone else's.

Watch this one. Count who clears both.

> **Takeaway** — The imperative returns here for the first time since cycle 2, and should read
> as a return to form rather than a habit.
> **Must be true** — The floors are set before any name appears, and they are two different
> bars on two different measures. *This* is the beat with thresholds in it, unlike the one
> before.
> **Would be a lie** — Any name visible in the setup.

### REVEAL

Five clear the left floor. Seven clear the right. Panels come together and two names are
standing in the middle.

Drake. And Travis Scott.

Travis belongs there, and he got there in fewer years. But look at the distance inside the
overlap. Drake's own-records number is close to three times Travis's, and his feature number is
forty percent bigger on top of that. First on both lists, at the far end of both, at the same
time.

> **Takeaway** — Two survive, and then the real point is the distance between the two who
> survived. The second movement matters more than the first.
> **Must be true** — Five, seven and two are counts of artists clearing bars. Travis qualifies
> legitimately and the piece says so.
> **Would be a lie** — Treating the two as equivalent because both cleared. Qualifying and
> leading are different, and the gap inside the overlap is the beat.

### RECONCILE

The range runs past lead and feature. A hundred and twelve top-ten entries. Forty-two as a lead
single. Twenty-eight as an album cut. Twenty-one as a guest. Eleven as an album. Then a
mixtape, a joint album, a compilation, an EP, a promo single.

Nine kinds of release. Top ten as all nine. Future and Nicki Minaj, next on that list, have
sixty-three top-tens combined.

> **Takeaway** — Breadth. Nine different ways of releasing a record, all of them charting.
> **Must be true** — The categories sum to a hundred and twelve. Nine kinds, top ten in every
> one. Sixty-three is two artists combined.
> **Would be a lie** — Ranking the nine categories, or implying the smaller ones are lesser
> achievements.

## CODA

Lil Baby is certifying his own records at a faster yearly clip than Drake has averaged across
the whole run — about twenty-two million units a year against twenty and a half. Kendrick, Post
Malone and Travis Scott have each put five lead singles at number one. Travis and Future are the
two biggest daily catalogs in the game after him, and adding a third name gets that group to
within seven percent.

Best rapper alive is an argument and this chart can't referee it. It counts plays. Nothing else
about it is interesting.

So, same chart as the start. Every dot, every play, every day. What share of that pile belongs
to one man in year seventeen?

Past the catalogs that barely register. Past the names you know. Past Future. Past Travis Scott.
To the dot at the top, which beats those two together and edges the next three together.

Fifteen percent. One play in every seven, across ninety-three rappers, every day.

Delete his biggest song and the ranking doesn't notice. His own records outweigh anybody's
everything. He's landed something on a chart every year for eighteen years running. Eleven
careers from his own two cover years, stacked together, beat him by three percent, and I'm
leaving that where it fell.

The rappers this should really be measured against — the ones whose seventeenth year happened
before streaming counted anything — can't go on this chart at all. There's no daily meter for
Jay-Z's year seventeen. So unprecedented only reaches back as far as the streaming era. That's
the honest version and I'll leave it there.

Seven hundred a second. It was seven hundred a second when I started counting, and it hasn't
stopped for the tour, the album cycle, the beef, any of it.

Year seventeen.

> **Takeaway** — The piece stops arguing a case and starts reading an account, limits
> included. The ending concedes rather than wins.
> **Must be true** — Fifteen percent is his share of the ninety-three-rapper daily total, the
> same population as the opening. The competitive figures at the top are real and are not
> being dismissed. The Jay-Z limitation is a genuine bound on the claim, not modesty.
> **Would be a lie** — A triumphant finish. Every closing device should be doing less than the
> one before it, ending on the tap rather than on him.
